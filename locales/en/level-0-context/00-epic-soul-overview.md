## 1. The Common Flow: From Misty Heaven and Earth to Bright Water

The entire epic consists of **6 Acts · 28 Chapters**, for ages **9–10**, closely following the unified version of *Giving birth to water* and preserving the poetic rhythm of the Muong people. This is not a series of separate stories but **a seamless life**: the land was born, water flooded, trees grew, cities were built, people lived, then people in turn learned to build houses, make fire, grow rice, cook wine, raise buffaloes, cast drums, divide the land — and finally learn to live at the price of greed.

1. **Act I — Opening Heaven and Earth and Origin of Race (Chapters 1–7):**
   From the space where there was no earth or sky, a great flood left the first land; From the ground grew a green tree with ninety branches, from the tree gave birth to Thu Tha and Thu Thien; The giant sycamore tree connected heaven and earth and then fell down, dividing it into one thousand nine hundred and nineteen sections; The body of ignorance gave birth to Lady Da Dan, two boys Cun Buom, and the eggs that hatched gave birth to families and peoples. At the end of Act, people learned to divide the year into the months to know how to wait for the seasons.

2. **Act II — Establishing Lang, Building Muong and Creating Culture (Chapters 8–16):**
   Muong needs a leader. Twice the procession of Dit Dang and Lang Ta Cai failed because demons blocked the way; The third time, Lang Cun Can dared to stand in the middle of the road, chase away ghosts, and protect the village. From here is a series of vocational training days: catching turtles to learn how to build stilt houses, asking for fire and water, going to Heaven to ask for rice seeds, learning to make wine yeast, asking for pigs and chickens, and then taming the buffalo back to the people.

3. **Act III — Starting a House, Casting a Bronze Drum, and Dividing the Land (Chapters 17–19):**
   Lang Cun Can broke the law when he married his biological sister, giving birth to twelve Ma Em boys; Then he married four other wives and gave birth to four sons. Muong cast bronze drums - a treasure for the whole community. But when the land was divided, the ugly piece of land the size of a monkey's ear was given to Lang Cun Khuong, sowing the seeds of injustice: Toong In lied, the two brothers fought, and the first death of a family member.

4. **Act IV — The Zhou Tree Cycle: Finding, Chopping, Building, and Burning (Chapters 20–23):**
   This is the heart of the epic. Tam Tach, the gentle keeper of the fields, found the stone chu tree with chu dong leaves and was told by the Vang Anh bird: don't tell the wolf king Ke Cho. He bragged, was tricked, and forced to lead the way. The tree does not fall because of a big ax, but because of a properly small ax blade; It could not be pulled by human strength but was pulled by a herd of otters — and by Tam Tach's own bones. The Chu house was built and then burned by Tam Tach's two children; From the unjust blood under the delusion tree, Moong Lo was born.

5. **Act V — Exorcising Beasts and Suppressing Rebellions (Chapters 24–27):**
   Muong hunted the biggest monster, divided its flesh among the tribes — and thereby explained why each tribe had its own weaving profession. Then the evil spread along the chain: the dog that ate Moong's lungs turned crazy, the fish that ate the dog's body turned crazy, the crow that ate the fish's body turned crazy. Finally, Toong In - the younger brother who died and was starved to death - returned twice as a field ghost enemy, then a lang ghost enemy, until he was defeated in the riverbed.

6. **Chapter VI — Triumph of Copper Lead Tam Quan Ke Cho (Chapter 28):**
   Muong is rich, prosperous, and has defeated the enemy. The last chapter is the three stages of a procession: taking care of the king's clothes (growing mulberries, raising silkworms, weaving silk), taking care of the throne (finding skilled workers), and then taking the king to Dong Chi. The rooster's words and the old man's words remind one thing: returning to Ke Cho but still remembering the ancestral land.

## 2. Symbolic Golden Thread Throughout

When preparing a narrative voice for 9–10 year old children, the narrator should keep his eyes on the six symbols repeated throughout all 28 Chapters:

- **Land and Water:** Land was born after the flood, water returned after the drought, then the flood rose again during the demonic war. The two words *land - water* are not only the names of the first two chapters but the names of the entire realm of life.
- **Tree:** Green tree with ninety branches → sycamore tree connecting heaven and earth → stone chu tree with chu dong leaves → mulberry tree raising silkworms. Each tree is a stepping stone to a new life; If you cut a tree the wrong way, it won't fall; if you burn a tree incorrectly, a monster will be born.
- **Fire:** The fire requested from the god Ta Cam Cot was extinguished by water → the trick of making fire with tinder → the fire burned the chu house for seventeen days. Fire is both precious and anger.
- **House:** Stilt house learned from turtles → Lang Cun Can house → spacious chu house → burned down chu house. The house is the measure of the Muong: if you build a house, you will gain the Muong, if you burn the house, you will lose the Muong.
- **Call and promise:** *Whoever doesn't have rice, pound the rice, whoever doesn't fish, pound the pond* - that adage is repeated before every big fight. The advice of the Oriole and the promise to the otters are the two decisive promises of Act IV.
- **The price of greed:** The later the epic, the clearer it becomes: precious things obtained by deception will be paid for with blood. Tam Tach died, the Chu house burned, Moong Lo was born, Toong In became a ghost - all connected by the word *greed*.

## 3. Reception Psychology & Storytelling Rhythm (Ages 9–10)

- **Psychological characteristics of ages 9–10:** Children begin to clearly distinguish between good and evil, fair and unfair, and are very sensitive to the fact that "those who do wrong must suffer". Children like lists, numbers, and strange names (one thousand nine hundred and nineteen fields, ninety rice dunes, three thousand seven nets) because they make the world feel real, measurable, and countable. At the same time, children need a safe place to stop after each stretch.
- **Standard tone & rhythm:**
  - **mo** voice: slow, steady, with a rhythm like tapping a gong. No drama, no drama. Each line of poetry is a breath; When the suffering ends, it stops for a beat.
  - Short sentences, specific images: *water surpasses seven U hills and overflows nine Bai hills*, *at dawn is equal to a pestle, at dawn is equal to a mat*. When retelling, keep the images true instead of explaining.
  - In dialogues, the voice changes are clear but slight: the voice of the Oriole bird is soft and slow; Lang Cun Khuong's voice was decisive; Tam Tach's voice was small and hesitant.
  - In the heavy sections (Tam Tach dies, Toong In is abandoned, Moong Lo is born), **tell briefly, no further description**. The pain lies in the whole village crying but no one dares to speak, not in the blood.
  - End each chapter with a calming rhythm: a question, a lingering image, or an appointment *"let's talk... to another episode"*.

## 4. How to Use the 5-Level Data Set When Practicing Telling

1. **Step 1 (Level 0):** Read this overview page and the upcoming Chapter page to understand the spirit, breathing, and how to read proper names before looking at the text.
2. **Step 2 (Level 1):** Read the entire text of each Chapter out loud 2–3 times to feel the syllables and rhythm; Remember the *Notes on voice* at the end of each chapter.
3. **Step 3 (Level 2):** Switch to the detailed outline of the Chapter being practiced, practice retelling without looking at the whole text, just sticking to important image and dialogue milestones.
4. **Step 4 (Level 3):** Use the summary outline of 28 Chapters to quickly review the connection, avoiding missing any stages.
5. **Step 5 (Level 4):** Look at the reminder pictures corresponding to each Chapter and freely retell it in your own living language.

> **Special note for Muong epics:** this is a *performed* text, not a reading story. The narrator should stand or sit up straight, his hands can lightly tap the table top to keep the rhythm, and allow short silences between the *words* - just like the old man shaking the bronze bell to take a breather.
