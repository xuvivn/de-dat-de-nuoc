# Level 0 — Chapter VI: Triumph of Copper Lead Tam Quan Ke Cho (1 Chapter)

> *"The land has been revealed, the lane has been established."* The whole epic boils down to that one sentence — and a final chapter to round it out.

---

## 1. Core Spiritual & Cultural Context

Act VI has only one chapter, but it is the **longest and most layered chapter** of the ending. It consists of three distinct parts, each part is an item that must be prepared before receiving the king:

1. **Worry about clothes and clothes:** The king doesn't have a red shirt, loose pants, brocade belt, or silk scarf. Muong had to study **mulberry growing, silkworm raising and silk weaving** - a completely new profession. Dat Cai Danh went to find Lang Bang's house in Khanh Hao Tang Ham land, learned how to pick mulberries, raise silkworms, boil cocoons, and spin yarn. The silkworm taught itself how to boil cocoons when the silk was stolen by the Dragon King fish. Then Lady Da Dan was welcomed to make clothes for the king.
2. **Worrying about the palanquin, worrying about the throne:** Muong refused to carry the king on a regular stretcher, and wanted to buy **a palanquin with dragon poles and a throne with a peacock's tail**. A boy from Lang's house went looking for a skilled worker, met a worker who was being beaten by King Mong Nong, saved him, and he accepted to make a palanquin for the throne on the condition that he would be given enough food.
3. **Bringing the king back to Dong Chien Tam Quan Ke Cho:** The whole village held a festival. Drums and gongs, colorful amulets, young men carrying palanquins, young and old following behind. The king sat on a palanquin and looked at the fields and docks, seeing **no more enemies, no more ghosts**. The rooster crowed to remind the king of his ancestral land. The old man asked to see the king off to Nhat Muong. The king broke off the tops of leaves and sprinkled them on the road, instructing people to make a living.

Worth noting: **the last chapter has no battles**. After all the blood and fire in Acts IV–V, the ending is **making clothes, building palanquins, and going on a journey**. This is a very beautiful choice of the epic: after eliminating the evil, the biggest thing left is **making life kind** - having clothes to wear, having a palanquin to go with, having instructions to leave behind.

And the last chapter is also where the epic is **reminder to myself**. The rooster's words list the entire journey: *remember the place of birth, the land of water, the place of the sycamore tree, the place of birth, the place of birth of fire, the place of birth of the house, the place of the stone chu tree, chu dong leaves...* That is a summary of 28 chapters, put in the mouth of a chicken. The narrator should read that passage as if going through the entire epic in one breath.

## 2. Characteristic Storytelling Rhythms (Ages 9–10)

- **Part 1 — Light, meticulous like a new profession:** Read **relaxedly, seeming curious**. The steps of growing mulberries, raising silkworms, boiling cocoons, and spinning yarn should be read **as instructions**, so that children can feel that this is a real profession. Then the silkworm said: **a small, clear, slightly strange voice** — because this was the only time the silkworm spoke.
- **Part 2 — Fun, a worker wants to eat:** Read **faster, mischievously**. The worker said *"I ate ten jars of rice, seven jars of wine in one gulp"* — read **naturally, not acted**. The part where the palanquin is built is painted in red and gold: **solid, bright**.
- **Part 3 — Slow, solemn, like a real procession:** This is the part that needs to **keep the most even and wide rhythm**. Then the king looked at the wharf field: **slow, warm**. Rooster's words: **understand every word, don't rush** — this is the first settling point. The old man's words: **very slow, very warm** — the second settling place. The last six sentences are read **evenly**, then pop up at *"Morning Muong, Bright Country!"* and **hold still for a beat** after that.

## 3. Touch Points & Metaphors for 9–10 Year Olds

- **Touch point:** Children love **the talking silkworm** and **how to boil the cocoon to pull out the thread** — a process that is both strange and can be done in the imagination. I like **the worker who demands food** because he is the last comedy character of the epic. And very fond of **the rooster crowing to the king** — a small animal saying a big thing.
- **Core metaphor:** Act VI closes with three things:
  1. **Muong matures when they know how to create beauty**, not only know how to survive: silk dresses, dragon palanquins, thrones.
  2. **When traveling far, you must remember your roots.** The king returned to Ke Cho, but the rooster reminded him *of the land and water*.
  3. **Stories are told to be passed on.** The last sentence *"whoever has heard it unfinished has heard it and heard it again, and will listen to it for generations to come"* is the handing of the epic from one teller to another listener — that is, to the child who is sitting and listening.
- **Where to keep:** Don't read the last chapter as a dry summary. It is **a procession**, there are drums, there are people walking, there are old people who want to go further. The narrator should let the children feel the atmosphere of the meeting before giving the final sentence. After the sentence *"The morning of the country, the morning of the country!"*, be silent for a beat — don't say anything else.
