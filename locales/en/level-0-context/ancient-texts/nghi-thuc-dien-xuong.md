# Muong Funeral Performance Ritual

> **Type of material:** Background material — ritual and narrative
> **Use when:** before the first telling; Practice resting between chapters
> **Source:** *Introduction* and *Appendix A* of the consolidated version

---

## 1. Space and time

- **Space:** in the middle of the funeral home's floor. The priest sat on a mat, in front of a hollowed-out wooden coffin, next to a nine-layer tray of five-colored sticky rice.
- **Time:** night. A large funeral can perform the entire *Earth and Water* in **twelve consecutive nights**. Every night there are some *that*, interrupted by chewing betel, drinking water and performing tricks.
- **Atmosphere:** both sacred and festive. The sadness is not pushed away, but is **calmed down** so that the whole community can sit together before the ancestors.

---

## 2. Mr. Mo's props

| Props | Role |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Peacock feather fan** | Hold one hand and wave to the rhythm of the song mo |
| **Small bronze bell** | Shake vigorously at the end of each *that* to change parts and take a breather |
| **Screen** | Spread across the floor, making the boundary between the living world and the living world
| **Nine-layer sticky rice tray** | Five colors of blue, red, purple, yellow, white — offerings to ancestors |
| **Arrangement of gongs, gongs, drums, trumpets** | Maintain the rhythm and open up the sound space for the lyrics

---

## 3. Structure a *that*

1. **Opening:** The monk recites the sentence, bringing the listener into the scene.
2. **Body:** told in poetic rhythm, with responses, lists, and choruses.
3. **Transition:** at the end of *that*, the sentence:

> *"That life is gone, that life has passed, I'll tell you that it's different."*

4. **Break:** Shake the bronze bell vigorously; chew betel, drink water; There may be an act.

> **Important:** the break is **not a throwaway**. It is part of the ritual. When telling your child, you should also keep the silence short — children need time for the images just told to settle.

---

## 4. The *buffers* during the break

Mourning nights are not just about mourning. Among *that* are bustling folk games:

- **Drum dance**
- **Cheo sacrifice**
- **Row and leak**
- **Traditional singing**

These games calm the grief, and turn the funeral night into a gathering night for the whole community. This is a very unique feature of Muong funerals: being able to laugh on the night of sending someone off, because death is placed in a larger story - the story of the country of birth and the people who continue to live.

---

## 5. Applicable when telling to children 9–10 years old

- **Keep the rhythm in three steps:** tell — sing the sentence — be silent for a beat.
- **You can use a small object to tap the rhythm** (no brass bell needed): tap lightly on the table at the end of each chapter.
- **Don't explain the ritual in the middle.** Tell first, explain later if the child asks.
- **Don't use a "spooky" voice.** Mo's voice is the voice of **old people telling stories to the whole village**, not the voice of scaring children.
- **End the story** with Mr. Mo's transition, then silence. That is the most natural way to close.

---

## 6. Three levels of meaning of rituals

1. **Belief layer:** Bringing the deceased's soul back to their ancestors, through *mo lych* and *mo luc*.
2. **Memory layer:** retains the entire sacred history of Muong in one long night — without books, memories are in the priest's voice.
3. **Community level:** the whole village sits together, listens together, cries together, laughs together, remembers who they are together.

Today's young narrator takes on the **second and third floors**: keeping memories and sitting together. Just one reminder at the beginning of the session is enough for children to feel the first level.
