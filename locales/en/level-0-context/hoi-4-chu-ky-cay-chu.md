# Level 0 — Act IV: The Cypress Tree Cycle — Find, Chop, Build and Burn (4 Chapters)

> *"The tree has no mouth, but makes a loud noise as it drifts."* That sentence is the heart of Act IV: a tree that can talk, know how to wait, and know how to ask for a price.

---

## 1. Core Spiritual & Cultural Context

Act IV is the **longest and heaviest act** of the epic, and also the most beautiful in terms of storytelling. Its four chapters are a complete cycle: **find — chop — build — burn**. A sacred tree was discovered, taken down, pulled back, built into a house, and then burned. From that fire and ashes a monster is born, opening Act V.

The center of Act IV is not the three Lang brothers, but **Tam Tach** - the gentle keeper of the fields, the only one who found the chu tree. He is not a hero in the strongest sense; He was lucky, then bragged, then deceived, then forced, then died. His death is **the Lang family's greatest sin**, and all of Act IV afterward — including Moong Lo — are its consequences.

Act IV is also the episode of **admonitions**. The Golden Bird advised: *When you get home, don't tell the king or the rich, don't tell the prince Ke Cho, then you will be rich forever*. Tam Tach did not keep his word. The servant who slept at the tree stump heard the ghost crying and told him: *I'm afraid the lotus ax blade is as small as a hand*. The Lang family followed that advice and cut down the tree. As for the otters, they asked for a very humble thing: *to be able to tear their nets apart in April and May, to be able to give birth to their babies at Lang's house*. Three instructions, three ways to behave: one word betrayed, one word heard, one word kept.

Culturally, Chapter IV explains **why the chu tree is a sacred tree of the Muong people**: *stone chu, dong chu leaves, brass flower, tin fruit* — the tree of family and community authority. And it is also a warning about precious things obtained with donkeys: the larger the house, the fiercer the fire.

## 2. Characteristic Storytelling Rhythms (Ages 9–10)

- **Chapter 20 — Adventure chapter, many stages:** Read in rhythm **on the road**: meeting ants → asking the buffalo herding child → meeting the strawberry picking girls → meeting the tu how → wrestling with Noong Pair → reaching the root of the chu → meeting Vang Anh → going home → being tricked. Each short stage has one question and one answer. Vang Anh's words must be read **at the latest in the chapter**, clearly understanding each word.
- **Chapter 21 — Two halves, two rhythms:** Half *chop chu* reads **chop, there is a hammer sound**; half *pull chu* read **heavy, as if there were strings in the voice**. Scene of Tam Tach's death: **one beat, so concise, indescribable**. Then scroll through dozens of places: reading them is like counting**, no need to press each name.
- **Chapter 22 — Changing tone mid-chapter:** The first half is **construction**: cheerful, firm, technical tone (workers carve columns, plan beams, build houses). The second half is a **love story**: the tone is **warm, slow**, especially in the part where Mr. Buddha appears. The part where the golden turtle was cooked and spread throughout the house: reading ** gradually brightens **.
- **Chapter 23 — The voice of injustice, then the voice of fire:** Opens with two children standing in the alley — **small, sad** voices. The part where he beats the drum and screams about the Lang family's crimes: his voice **pops out**, angry but not crazy. The part where the old woman teaches how to tie straw to the cat's tail: her voice is **calm, almost gentle** - because it's calm, it's scary. Burning fire: **fast, intense** rhythm. Moong Lo is born: **slows down, just enough horror**, then lets go to open Act V.

## 3. Touch Points & Metaphors for 9–10 Year Olds

- **Touching point:** Children like the most **Noong Pair** - the orangutan gods want to wrestle each other, if they lose, they agree to be the younger brother, and the four wrestling matches are considered a sports match. Also really like **chopping tip**: an ax as big as a boat plank won't work, only an ax blade as small as a hand can cut down a tree. This is one of the epic's best images: **right is more important than big**. And the **herd of otters** are characters that young people will love: they don't ask for gold or silver, they just ask to live on the wharf.
- **Core metaphor:** Act IV teaches three things:
  1. **The advice is to keep.** Tam Tach lost everything because of a boast.
  2. **Strength does not lie in size.** The chu tree fell because of the small ax blade; The tree leaves because of the otter herd.
  3. **Evil is not born out of thin air — it is paid for.** Moong Lo is not a monster that fell from the sky; It was born from the blood of two children who were treated like dogs.
- **Where to keep:** This is an episode with a lot of heavy content. The narrator must **keep a distance**: tell Tam Tach's death as something Muong did and Muong cried, without describing blood; Tell Moong Lo's birth as a consequence, not as a horror scene. Children 9–10 years old need to understand *why*, not *how*. In particular, don't let children see Tam Tach's two children as "bad guys": they are two starved children at the celebration of their father's killers.
