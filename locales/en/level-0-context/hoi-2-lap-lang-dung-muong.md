# Level 0 — Act II: Establishing Lang, Building Muong and Creating Culture (9 Chapters)

> *"Muong has land, water, and people - but no leader, no house, no fire, no rice."* Act II is the nine stages of vocational training of a community that has just formed.

---

## 1. Core Spiritual & Cultural Context

If Act I answers the question *"where does the world come from"*, then Act II answers the question *"how to live"*. This is the era of **material culture**: choosing a leader, building a stilt house, finding fire, finding water, growing rice, making wine, raising pigs and chickens, taming buffaloes. Each chapter is a profession, and each profession is told as a **begging trip** — going to the sky, going down to the river, going to the forest, going to another village.

The structure of Act II is very beautiful: **three lang processions** opens like a chorus. Dit Dang and Lang Ta Cai both demanded heavy offerings and were interrupted by a demon spell; Only Lang Cun Can - a humble man who claimed to have "strength as strong as a fig, talent as small as a cassia seed"* - dared to stand in the middle of the road, and the dragon had to hide. The lesson lies in this: **the leader of the Muong is not the one who demands the most, but the one who dares to stand up**.

After that, there was a series of "searching" after each other: the turtle taught how to build a house → the fire god Ta Cam Cot → the Rice Fairy → Lang Kham Dam taught how to make yeast → Mother Hung Mu La gave pigs and chickens → mother Buffalo Duc brought her children back to her people. It is worth noting: **precious items are often not retrieved intact**. Visiting Cu Linh, spilling water to extinguish the fire; Tun Mun also lost his fire but learned *trick*; Miss Dat Cai Đình forgot her gift and had to come back. The epic says very clearly: **it's not the wealth that can be kept, but the way to make it**.

Act II also lays the foundation for later rules. The story of the buffalo having to choose to live with people, the story of Tiger Lang cutting off the relationship between sisters, the story of Mrs. Da Dan cursing the buffalo to eat rice - all are the first traces of a world with boundaries: what belongs to people, what belongs to the forest.

## 2. Characteristic Storytelling Rhythms (Ages 9–10)

- **Chapters 8–10 — Three repetitions, one repetition:** Read the same rhythm, but each time a little faster, because the child is familiar with the way. Dit Easy and Lang Ta Cai's voices are **formal but a bit flamboyant**; Lang Cun Can's voice is **humble, firm**. The scene where the ghost appears: lower your voice, tell it briefly, and don't make it scary.
- **Chapter 11 — Two turtles, two voices:** The deep turtle spoke in a **begging, tactful** voice; The golden turtle spoke in a **calm, instructive** voice. The scene of the black turtle being punished in just one beat is indescribable.
- **Chapter 12 — The voice of loss and gain:** Viet Cu Linh's trip is told **fast, funny, a bit awkward**; Tun Mun's trip is told **slower in the tips section**, because that's the memorable part.
- **Chapter 13 — Seasons:** This is the most complete chapter on agriculture. The last paragraph reads according to the rhythm of the month: *April scatters seedlings, July young rice, August fields...* — evenly, slowly, like a circle.
- **Chapters 14–15 — Light, happy tone:** Two chapters break after the rice chapter. The voices of the two **mischievous** children of Pap Pam; Mrs. Hung's voice, Mrs. La's voice, was warm and a bit playful. This is where children can laugh.
- **Chapter 16 — The saddest voice Chapter:** Tell **calmly, without judgment**. The scene where Tiger Lang dies in just one beat. The last sentence about the mark engraved on the buffalo horn is read slowly, like a reminder that remains for the next generation.

## 3. Touch Points & Metaphors for 9–10 Year Olds

- **Touching point:** Children like **three times to pick up a lang** the most because there is a hidden comedy: every man wants to eat before accepting the job, then both men lose to the ghost. Also very close to the children were **two Pap Pam buffalo herders** - lost in the fun festival, had a full meal, and then learned the secret to making yeast. And **the buffalo** is the cutest young character in Act: he's not ugly, he just has to choose.
- **Core metaphor:** Every job in Act II comes from a **begging for a price**. Fire can be obtained, but you must learn the trick; If you get the rice, you must remember to be grateful to Lady Dat Cai Danh; You can build a house, but you have to listen to the turtle. Children 9–10 years old understand this very well through the experience of learning a skill: **nothing can be used without effort**.
- **Place to keep:** Keep Lang Cun Can's **humility as the center, don't let children miss Lang just because he has many wives (that belongs in Act III). And keep the Buffalo chapter a little sad — don't make it a joke.
