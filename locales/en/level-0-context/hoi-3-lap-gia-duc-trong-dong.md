# Level 0 — Act III: Establishing a House, Casting a Bronze Drum, and Dividing the Land (3 Chapters)

> *"Muong now has a house, fire, rice, and buffalo. Now it needs a family — and a treasure for the whole country to listen to."* Act III has only three chapters but is the heaviest turning point of the first half of the epic.

---

## 1. Core Spiritual & Cultural Context

The three chapters of Act III are three big things: **establishing a house**, **casting bronze drums**, **dividing land**. Every thing has a bright side and a dark side.

**Bright side:** Lang Cun Can got married, had four sons, and had descendants. Muong cast a bronze drum — *khau lac tru bronze* — a treasure symbolizing the sacred authority of the Lang family, which later became known as *drum for worshiping ancestors*. The whole village learned a new profession: bronze casting.

**Dark side:** Lang Cun Can broke the law when he wanted to marry his younger brother, Va Hai Kip. The whole village protested, Heaven sent Thunder down, and from that crime were born twelve Demons who guarded twelve water stations, forcing people into madness. When it came time to divide the land, Lang Cun Khuong - the best son - only got a piece of land as small as a monkey's ear or a cat's ear. From that land grew injustice, lies, and Toong In's death.

Act III is therefore the **laying down of the law** chapter. It says two things very clearly that 9–10 year old children understand immediately: **there are things you cannot do, even if you are the leader**; and **if the share is unfair, that injustice will return**. The narrator doesn't need to preach — just tell the correct order of cause and effect.

Also in Act III, for the first time, the epic has **a death of a member of the Lang family**. Toong In died because of his crime, but that death will return twice more in Act V - becoming a ghost field, then becoming a ghost may ma lang. Therefore, chapter 19 must be told **concisely and clearly**, so that children remember *why* he died, not *how* he died.

## 2. Characteristic Storytelling Rhythms (Ages 9–10)

- **Chapter 17 - The voice is tense, then quiet:** Opens with a very real story: *Lang's family lacks people to raise pigs and chickens, spread blankets and mats*. Then it tenses up in the protest part - Muong's voice must be **decisive, in unison**, without hesitation. The Thunder falls: fast, compact. The last part takes Va Hai Kip down to the big beach: **slow, silent, indescribable**.
- **Chapter 18 — Light voice, lots of smiles:** This is the longest but lightest chapter of the Chapter. Seven men dream of going to the alley and encountering bad omens - read **fast, evenly, like counting**; he dreamed of Poor and ragged but prosperous - read **slowly, mischievously**. The grandmother interrupting the wedding invitation is where the child laughs the loudest; Read **naturally, like she really said**.
- **Chapter 19 — Three distinct beats:**
  1. *Drum casting:* **excited, technical** voice, a bit nervous when the drum is not sharp three times.
  2. *Dividing the land:* voice **gradually descends**, each part of the land has a beat; Cun Khuong's part must be read **small and slow** so that children can feel the sadness.
  3. *Toong In and death:* tone **calm, non-judgmental**; The buffalo offering sentence at the end of the chapter reads **slowly and warmly**, like a closing statement.

---

## 3. Touch Points & Metaphors for 9–10 Year Olds

- **Touching point:** Children really like **seven dreams** — the same bad omen seven times, like a repeating game that children can predict. Also like **casting bronze drums**: three times the bronze is poured without being sharp, then the worker chews red ginger and purple turmeric and sprays it into the mold, the drum takes shape - a detail that is both strange and memorable. And **the piece of land made of monkey ears and cat ears** is an image that children immediately understand: so small that it's funny, but not laughable.
- **Core metaphor:** Two big questions of Act III:
  1. *Is there anything a leader is allowed to do?* Muong's answer is **no**.
  2. *What if the shares are not divided equally?* The epic's answer is that **injustice does not stay still**.
- **Where to keep:** Chapter 17 has sensitive content (sibling marriage). Tell **briefly, keep the narrator's distance**, focus on Muong's reaction and consequences, do not go into details. Chapter 19 has a scene where Toong In dies: just one beat, no blood, and must be kept to show children that *he did the wrong thing first* — death is a consequence, not a distraction.
