# Level 0 — Act V: Eliminate Beasts and Suppress Demonic Rebellions (4 Chapters)

> *"Fight the ghosts and return them to the forest"* — but to be able to fight them, you must understand that there are ghosts who are your own people.

---

## 1. Core Spiritual & Cultural Context

Act V is an act of **battle**, but not a battle with an external enemy. Its four chapters are four layers of evil, each layer growing out of the previous layer:

1. **Giant Moon** — a monster born from the blood of Tam Tach and Tach under the si tree. The first evil is **the consequence of the Lang family's crime**.
2. **Mad dog — mad fish — mad crow** — evil **spreads along the chain**: dog eats Moong lungs, fish eats dog corpse, crow eats fish corpse. None of them were "evil" from the start; they only eat something that is infected.
3. **Ghost enemy in the field** — Toong In, the younger brother who died, returned because of **starvation and incense smoke**. The third evil is **unjust soul**.
4. **The enemy is lucky, the enemy is evil** — Toong In returned for the last time, was ordained by King Thuy Phu, and died completely in the riverbed. The final evil is **the younger brother is not accepted**.

Chapter 24 is also one of the most beautiful pages of the epic: after killing Moong, the Muong divided the skin between the ethnic groups, and **each tribe received a different part of the skin so they had a different weaving craft** - the Lao people took the skin from the hips so they weaved it beautifully, the Muong Trong people took the skin from the back so they embroidered the horse's back, the Tay people who came late took the skin from the tail so they only embroidered bee worms, the Muong people kept the skin on their chest so they wove the head of a deer skirt. This is how the epic explains the differences between the races **without ever saying one is superior to the other**: all are brothers sharing the same beast.

Act V is also the **most difficult episode in terms of narrative voice**, because it puts the narrator in the position of having to both praise the Lang family's victory and let the children hear that **the Lang family was wrong from the beginning**. A good storyteller is one who can hold both at the same time.

## 2. Characteristic Storytelling Rhythms (Ages 9–10)

- **Chapter 24 — Counting voice, then dividing voice:** The part where Moong grows up reads **as if counting**: Moong one, Moong two... to Moong ten; Each name has a beat, gradually getting faster. Hunting part: **pull, there is the sound of a gong and the sound of a dog**. The skin part: **changes completely to a happy, even tone**, like a closing pride — and this is where you should slow down so that children can hear each clan clearly.
- **Chapter 25 — Fast, concise, somewhat humorous voice:** Three paragraphs follow each other like three short stories. The scene where the fish attacks the three brothers: **chaotic, fast**; the part where the crow calls *"brain — crow — ah — quack quack"*: **read correctly, make children laugh**; But laughter must come **after** fear.
- **Chapter 26 — The Voice of the Abandoned One:** Opens with Toong In's words of blame — read **slowly, sadly, not harshly**. This is where the narrator is most likely to make a mistake: if he reads in an evil tone, children will not understand why he is blaming him. The three brothers' three questions were a **short comedy break** before the match. The battle: **rammed, then subsided** when Toong In dived into the water.
- **Chapter 27 — Last voice:** Toong Í appears half-human, half-dragon — read **cold, distant**. Cun Tang's voice must be **hard, decisive, not accepting you**. Dragon death scene: **compact**. The story of the crow carrying the cactus: **slowly, warmly**, as a form of gratitude — and this is the rhythm that closes Act V.

## 3. Touch Points & Metaphors for 9–10 Year Olds

- **Touch point:** Children love **Giant Moon grows up by name** — as a counting game with pictures. Liked the **crazy fish fight** because it was chaotic and funny (the three brothers lost their knives and slingshots, Cun Tang drank river water). And like the **crow** at the end of the Act: the smallest animal is the most meaningful, the King of Heaven repays his favor with a very specific law.
- **Core metaphor:** Act V teaches that **evil is not always a stranger**. Moong Lo was born from unjust blood; crazy fish born from dog corpses; Toong In is my younger brother. Children 9–10 years old are old enough to understand: **if you want to eliminate evil, you have to know where it comes from** — and sometimes you have to look at your own mistakes first.
- **Place to keep:** Toong In **is not a simple villain**. He lied, he waged war, but he was also starved of incense and smoke after death - a real sadness in Muong beliefs. The narrator needs to let the children hear **his blame** as a pain, then tell the story of the battle. The death scenes (Moong, fish, dragon) are narrated **concisely, without describing blood**.
