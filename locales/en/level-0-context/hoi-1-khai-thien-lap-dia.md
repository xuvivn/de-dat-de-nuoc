# Level 0 — Act I: Opening Heaven and Earth and Origin of Race (7 Chapters)

> *"There is no earth under the earth, there is no heaven in the sky"* — that opening sentence is not a description of an empty scene, but an invitation to enter a world that is preparing to be born.

---

## 1. Core Spiritual & Cultural Context

Act I is **the origin myth of the Muong people**, told exactly the way the ancients imagined: the world was not created in seven days, but **was born layer by layer**, like a mother giving birth. First of all, there is a vague, undefined space; Then a great flood of water receded, leaving the land behind; Earth gives birth to trees; The tree gave birth to two ancestors; The sycamore tree connects heaven and earth and then falls; The body of ignorance gave birth to Lady Da Dan; and from the last eggs hatched the families, the peoples, the Lang brothers.

The special thing about Act I is that **there is no outside creator**. The King of Heaven exists, the God of Thunder exists, but they are also characters in the story, sometimes jealous, sometimes afraid, sometimes having to negotiate with the world. Life is born from **water, soil, trees, eggs and blood** — things very close to the farming life of the Muong people. Therefore, Act I is both sacred and very "realistic": the sycamore tree is so big that it covers the horizon, but the reason the King of Heaven wants to destroy it is very ordinary - *on earth drying rice does not dry, drying vegetables does not dry*.

This period is also the first **map of Muong country**. When the sycamore tree falls, each branch falls into a Muong, and the list of Muong names is as long as a map read out in rhythm. Muong people listen to Hoi I not only to know old stories, but to hear the name of their land in it.

## 2. Characteristic Storytelling Rhythms (Ages 9–10)

- **Chapter 1 — Inviting voice:** Slow, resonant, like an old person spreading a mat in the middle of the house. The three stanzas *"Want..."* should be read evenly like tapping; When you get to the part *"under the ground there is no land"*, lower your voice, slow down, and let the space appear on its own.
- **Chapters 2–3 — The Voice of Water:** Read according to the rise and fall of the water: tight when the water overflows, slack when the water recedes, dry when nine months of drought. The sentence *"nine hills flow into one stream, nine streams flow into one river"* read quickly and then let go.
- **Chapters 4–5 — Voices of trees and rocks:** Chapter 4 is the voice telling a story about a big tree — slowly, with a bit of regret. Chapter 5 is the **counting** voice: each branch has a direction, read evenly like reading a map, occasionally pressing a name for children to remember.
- **Chapter 6 — Four clear beats:** Lady Da Dan (warm, humorous) → two fairies (clear, light) → Chim Tung Chim Tot (sad, then bright) → eggs hatch (rushed, many voices). This is the longest chapter of Act; should be divided into four breaks.
- **Chapter 7 — The voice counting the months:** even, leisurely, like a calendar hanging on the wall. The chapter ends with the light title *Diet Easy*, opening to Act II.

## 3. Touch Points & Metaphors for 9–10 Year Olds

- **Touching points:** Children really like the details of **trees growing like crazy** - at night it's the size of a pestle, in the morning it's the size of a mat, and in the afternoon it's the size of a four-by-three stick. This is an image that children can draw right away. Likewise, the story of **eggs hatching into nations** makes children feel something great in a very concrete way: everyone is brothers in the same nest.
- **Core metaphor:** Act I teaches that **the world is not a given but a process**. Land needs water to recede to become land; The tree needs to die to be born; The sycamore tree has to fall for the Muong to exist. Children 9–10 years old are old enough to feel: some things are lost to pave the way for something else to be born - but the narrator **doesn't need to say that idea**, just needs to tell the story in the right rhythm for it to linger on its own.
- **Places to keep:** Respect the sacredness of the sycamore tree and Chieng's eggs. No laughing, no explanations with modern science in the middle of the story. If the child asks, you can say: *"The Muong people tell it like that, and in that story there is a truth about how we were born from land and water."*
